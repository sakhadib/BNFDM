\title{Literal Capture in Typed Decision Models: A Behavioural Audit of\\
Calibration, Option-Order Stability and Figurative Meaning\\
in Bangla Idioms}

\author{Anonymous Submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Decision models --- non-autoregressive scorers that return a calibrated
probability over caller-supplied options and emit no text --- are increasingly
deployed as routers, classifiers and guardrails. They generate nothing, so the
usual explainability toolkit of rationales, chain-of-thought and token
attribution does not apply, and idioms make token-level attribution ill-posed in
principle: a non-compositional expression has no word whose removal is
informative. We therefore audit three such models entirely through
\emph{interventions on the option set}, using 10{,}361 expert-annotated Bangla
idioms that ship with both a gold figurative gloss and a gold literal gloss. Our
instrument holds the item, the instruction and $k$ fixed and varies only the
distractor family, which isolates one failure mode at a time and needs no new
annotation. Three results hold across an encoder scorer, a decoder scorer and a
closed commercial API. First, \emph{literal capture} is severe and does not
decrease with capability: inserting the idiom's own literal gloss drives accuracy
far below chance, and the model that is better on every other condition is the
more captured one. Second, confidence is anti-diagnostic under this shift: both open models are more
confident when captured than when correct, and confidence-gated selective
prediction is worse than random gating. Third, option-order sensitivity
dissociates cleanly by architecture:
83.7\% versus 10.7\% of items change their chosen gloss across the 24 orderings
of the same four options. A semantics-free control shows the strongest model
reporting 0.815 mean confidence at 0.254 accuracy. We release all artifacts.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A \emph{decision model} is given a question and a list of candidate options and
returns a probability distribution over exactly those options. It is not
autoregressive, it does not sample, and it produces no text. This class has
grown quickly as infrastructure --- routing, intent classification, content
gating, tool selection --- precisely because it is cheap, bounded and returns a
number a caller can threshold on. An ecosystem analysis of 2{,}170 public
projects finds it already embedded as a reusable decision component across
domains \citep{ling2026wild}, and proposals now place these models at the
adjudication points of autonomous security harnesses
\citep{santos2026pentest}. The threshold is load-bearing.

That bounded interface is also an explainability problem. There is no rationale
to inspect, no chain of thought to measure for faithfulness
\citep{jacovi2020faithfulness,turpin2023unfaithful}, and in the commercial case
no weights at all. What is left is the behavioural surface: the caller controls
the question and the option list, and sees a probability vector. Any audit of
this class has to be built out of that.

The obvious fallback --- attributing the decision to input tokens
\citep{ribeiro2016lime,lundberg2017shap} --- is not merely unreliable here
\citep{jain2019attention,adebayo2018sanity,hooker2019roar}; for our domain it is
ill-posed. An idiom is non-compositional by definition
\citep{nunberg1994idioms}: the words of \emph{kick the bucket} do not carry
fractions of its meaning, so a Shapley value over them attributes a quantity
that does not exist. The expression has to be intervened on as a unit, or not at
all.

We take the option set as the intervention surface instead. Bangla
\emph{b\=agdh\=ar\=a} (idioms) are well suited to this because the expert
resource we use ships, for each idiom, both a gold figurative meaning and a gold
\emph{literal} gloss --- a ready-made, human-certified counterfactual distractor
that no annotation effort of ours produced. Dropping that gloss into the option
list turns a benchmark question into a targeted probe: does the model read the
expression as an idiom, or as its words?

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item A meaning-choice instrument for decision models in which the
\emph{distractor family} is the independent variable and everything else is held
fixed, requiring no new annotation (\S\ref{sec:instrument}).
\item \textbf{Literal capture does not decrease with capability.} We define the
Literal Attraction Rate and show a double dissociation: the model that is better
on every non-literal condition is substantially more captured
(\S\ref{sec:capture}).
\item \textbf{Confidence is anti-diagnostic under this shift}, across three
different calibration philosophies, with confidence-gated selective prediction
worse than random gating (\S\ref{sec:confidence}).
\item \textbf{Option-order dependence dissociates by architecture}, cleanly and
at scale, with a methodological consequence for anyone evaluating this class
(\S\ref{sec:order}).
\item A semantics-free control that bounds what each model reports when there is
provably nothing to report (\S\ref{sec:nosignal}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{The decision-model class.} The evaluation literature here is still
forming: an audit of 28 papers appearing within nine days of the first widely
used release finds that the typed readout has shown no independent accuracy
advantage, with the gains sitting in latency, cost and cascade placement
\citep{tang2026audit}. Independent evaluations agree on the shape --- competitive
rubric judging at 29--325$\times$ lower cost \citep{rao2026rubric}, a median
11.6 F1 deficit at 44$\times$ lower cost across 18 social-science tasks
\citep{ibrahim2026evaluating}, zero-shot alignment-failure detection from one
calibrated call \citep{guo2026justask}, and efficiency from sharing encoded
context \citep{yu2026visualjev}.

Two results bear directly on ours. \citet{ibrahim2026evaluating} report
decision-model \emph{calibration exceeding most LLM baselines} and recommend a
cheap first stage that routes uncertain items onward; \citet{li2026jevjudge}
build that system, accepting high-confidence verdicts and escalating the rest.
Both are load-bearing on the returned confidence being diagnostic, and
\S\ref{sec:confidence}--\S\ref{sec:nosignal} give the conditions under which it
is not --- a boundary condition on the pattern, not a contradiction of it.
Concurrently, \citet{sun2026typesafe} show these models follow an option's
\emph{name} rather than the definition bound to it, type-safety masking the
failure because every output stays well-formed, and \citet{xu2026jevout} flip
61.4\% of correct decisions with benign context. \S\ref{sec:bagofwords} and
\S\ref{sec:unit} meet both from the opposite direction.

\paragraph{Idioms in NLP.} Idiom resources and tasks are well established
\citep{haagsma2020magpie,tayyarmadabushi2022semeval,chakrabarty2022flute},
including for Bangla \citep{sakhawat2026bagdhara}, and the difficulty of
figurative language for distributional models is long-noted
\citep{shwartz2019pain}. Psycholinguistics frames the phenomenon as competition
between a literal and a figurative route whose resolution depends on
decomposability and context
\citep{cacciari1988comprehension,gibbs1989kick,titone1999compositional}. Our
\Dlit{} intervention is a deliberate behavioural analogue of that competition:
we place both routes in the option list and read off which one wins.

\paragraph{Evaluating option scorers.} Option-order sensitivity and selection
bias are documented for prompted generative LMs
\citep{pezeshkpour2024order,wei2024selection,zheng2024robust,zhao2021calibrate}.
We are not aware of a comparison of option-marker against independent-option
scoring in small non-autoregressive models over the full permutation set, which
is what \S\ref{sec:order} provides.

\paragraph{Calibration and selective prediction.} Proper scoring and calibration
measurement are standard
\citep{brier1950verification,gneiting2007proper,guo2017calibration,nixon2019measuring,kumar2019verified},
as is the observation that calibration degrades under distribution shift
\citep{ovadia2019trust,jiang2021know}. Selective prediction gives the operational
test \citep{geifman2017selective,geifman2019selectivenet,traub2024selective}. We
report the combination that matters for deployment: accuracy \emph{decreasing}
with confidence, and a confidence gate underperforming a random one.

\paragraph{Behavioural testing.} Our design follows behavioural checklists,
contrast sets and counterfactual minimal pairs
\citep{ribeiro2020checklist,gardner2020contrast,kaushik2020difference,naik2018stress,jia2017adversarial};
the contribution is carrying that tradition into a model class with no text
output, where it is one of the few instruments left.

\paragraph{Low-resource evaluation.} Bangla has strong encoders and corpora
\citep{bhattacharjee2022banglabert,kakwani2020indicnlpsuite}, and cross-lingual
benchmarks establish the transfer gap
\citep{hu2020xtreme,ruder2021xtremer,adelani2021masakhaner};
\S\ref{sec:crosslingual} localises our failure to the Bangla side rather than to
figurativeness.

\section{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

\paragraph{Source corpus.} We use \emph{Bangla Bagdhara}
\citep{sakhawat2026bagdhara}, a corpus of 10{,}361 Bangla (Bengali) idioms
annotated under a 19-field schema by expert consensus.\footnote{Our load yields
10{,}359 rows; the two-row gap is an unresolved parsing discrepancy and affects
no reported figure. Of these, 8{,}876 carry every field the instrument
requires.} Each row carries the idiom,
a gold figurative meaning in Bangla, an expert literal gloss, semantic tags,
alternative surface forms certified as meaning-preserving, and a gold carrier
sentence. We add no annotation; every intervention below is assembled from
fields that already exist.

Its own benchmark on the same underlying task reports no model among 30
multilingual and instruction-tuned LLMs above 50\% accuracy, against 83.4\% for
human annotators. Option construction differs, so those figures are not directly
comparable to ours, but they locate the difficulty in the material rather than
in our instrument.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:instrument}

\subsection{The meaning-choice task}

Given an idiom, choose its gold figurative meaning from $k=4$
candidates. Chance is $0.250$ throughout. The \textbf{distractor family is the
independent variable}; the item, the instruction, $k$, and per-item option-order
randomisation (seeded by item id) are held fixed across families, so a paired
test across families is a test on byte-identical questions.

\subsection{Distractor families}

\Drand{} draws other idioms' figurative glosses at random. \Dsurf{} draws glosses
that share surface tokens with the gold. \Dsem{} draws glosses from idioms
sharing semantic tags. \Dlit{} replaces one distractor with the idiom's
\emph{own} expert literal gloss. \Dhard{} combines all three pressures.

\subsection{Literal Attraction Rate}

For the families containing a literal
gloss, let $\ell_i$ be the literal option and $g_i$ the gold:
\[
\LAR = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}\!\left[\arg\max_o p_i(o) = \ell_i\right].
\]
\LAR{} is not $1-\text{accuracy}$: it isolates the share of errors that are the
specific error of reading the idiom literally, and it is bounded below by nothing
--- a model that never reads literally scores $0$ while still being wrong.

\subsection{Seven interventions}

E1 is the distractor ladder above. E2 swaps the
idiom for a certified synonymous surface form. E3 perturbs the idiom as a unit
(shuffle, reverse, delete one word, substitute one word, embed in its carrier
sentence) with the options held fixed. E4 swaps the task language. E5 measures
calibration and selective prediction. E6 ablates the option text itself
(truncate, scramble, translate, strip to bare labels). E7 runs all $4!=24$
orderings of a fixed option set.

\subsection{Statistics and pairing}

All intervals are 95\% percentile bootstrap over items
(2{,}000 resamples). Paired comparisons use McNemar's exact test. ECE uses 15
equal-width bins; we also report AURC against a random-gate baseline.

\paragraph{Pairing verification.} The two open arms were run in separate
accounts from independent notebooks, each reconstructing the corpus and the task
instrument from a hardcoded contract. On all 4{,}440 overlapping rows the gold
key, gold position, idiom string and literal-distractor key agree at $1.000$.
Every paired test below is therefore on identical questions, not on
re-derivations that happen to look alike.

\subsection{Models}

We audit three decision models spanning the design space (Table~\ref{tab:models}).
\textbf{Laya-ml} is a bidirectional encoder with an option-marker scorer: all
options are encoded together and scored from marker positions.
\textbf{Lod-lille} is a decoder backbone with independent-option scoring, where
each option attends only to the shared state, the question and itself from the
same start position --- order-independent by construction in \texttt{fp32}.
\textbf{Jev-1.13} is a closed commercial model behind a decisions API; we know
its interface and nothing else. It is not multilingual by documentation, which
we exploit rather than regret: it provides the capability contrast that makes
\S\ref{sec:capture} a dissociation rather than an anecdote.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Literal capture does not decrease with capability}
\label{sec:capture}

Table~\ref{tab:ladder} and Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} give the ladder for the two
open arms on the 888 shared items. Lod-lille is the stronger model on every
non-literal condition, by up to $+0.133$. Insert the idiom's own literal gloss
and the ordering \emph{reverses}: Lod-lille falls to $0.066$, less than half of
Laya-ml's already-below-chance $0.151$, and barely a quarter of chance. The same
reversal holds for \Dhard.

This is the central result: \LAR{} measures something accuracy does not, so a
model can be selected as ``better'' on a conventional benchmark while being
strictly worse at what that benchmark proxies for. The error is not diffuse ---
the decomposition under \Dlit{} is gold / literal / other $= 0.151$ / $0.644$ /
$0.207$ for Laya-ml and $0.066$ / $0.885$ / $0.048$ for Lod-lille, and on the
clearest items Laya-ml places $p = 1.000$ on the literal gloss.

\paragraph{A confound we do not hide.} Jev-1.13 reaches $\LAR = 0.047$, two
orders of magnitude below the open arms. That number is \emph{English}. The
shared literal pool exists only for the 3{,}265 items with an English literal
gloss, and the commercial arm ran it in English while the open arms ran it in
Bangla. Figure~\ref{fig:matrix} draws the full language $\times$ model matrix
including the two cells never run, so the shape of the missing experiment is
visible rather than inferred. The pool is also not neutral: Laya-ml's in-pool
\LAR{} is $0.747$ against $0.583$ out of pool while its \Drand{} accuracy
differs by only $0.008$, so items with an English literal gloss are the more
literally-attracting ones.

\subsection{Confidence is anti-diagnostic}
\label{sec:confidence}

split by outcome, on the literal-bearing families. Both open models are
\emph{more confident when captured than when correct}, Lod-lille extremely so
($0.787$ against $0.453$). The reliability curves in
Figure~\ref{fig:reliability} slope \emph{downward} under \Dlit{} in both.

Selective prediction therefore inverts. Restricting Laya-ml's \Dlit{} to the most
confident 5\% of items gives $0.007$ accuracy against $0.151$ at full coverage,
and the confidence-gated AURC is worse than a random gate for both open arms. The
failure is not an artifact of one calibration philosophy: Laya-ml ships fitted
per-bucket temperatures, Lod-lille deliberately ships none, and Lod-lille further
ships a \emph{separate confidence head} whose documented purpose is estimating
whether the answer is in the option list at all. The head does not rescue it ---
mean head confidence is $0.631$ on captured items against $0.295$ on correct
ones, the same inversion.

Jev-1.13 is the interesting exception, and it is only an exception in one
direction. Its reliability is near-ideal in English (ECE $0.005$--$0.047$) and
its confidence-gated AURC beats random by an order of magnitude. That makes
\S\ref{sec:nosignal} harder to dismiss, not easier.

This does not contradict \citet{ibrahim2026evaluating}, who find decision-model
calibration better than most LLM baselines --- we reproduce that for the
commercial arm on its own language. The claim is narrower: in-distribution
calibration does not transfer to a targeted distractor shift, and it fails in
the costly direction. A confidence-gated cascade \citep{li2026jevjudge} accepts
the high-confidence tail without review, and on the literal-bearing families
that tail is where the open models are \emph{least} accurate, so the gate routes
exactly the wrong items past the escalation step.

\subsection{A semantics-free control}
\label{sec:nosignal}

The \textsc{o4} ablation replaces every option's text with a bare label. No
semantic signal remains, so accuracy is pinned at chance by construction. Whatever
confidence survives is therefore pure overconfidence, with no modelling
assumption required to interpret it. Figure~\ref{fig:nosignal} reports it.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig5_no_signal_control.pdf}
\caption{The semantics-free control. Per model, the \textbf{grey} bar is accuracy
(with 95\% CI) and the \textbf{coloured} bar is mean reported confidence; the
black dashed line is chance. Because the options carry no information, accuracy
is at chance by construction and the gap between the two bars is pure
overconfidence: $0.250$ for Laya-ml, $0.049$ for Lod-lille, and $0.561$ for
Jev-1.13 --- which reports $0.815$ confidence while answering at $0.254$.
Jev-1.13's condition is English.}
\label{fig:nosignal}
\end{figure}

The ordering reverses against Table~\ref{tab:conf}. The best-calibrated model
under real options is the worst when the options carry nothing: Jev-1.13 reports
$0.815$ mean confidence at $0.254$ accuracy, an ECE of $0.561$. Lod-lille, the
weakest of the three on accuracy, is the only one whose reported confidence
collapses appropriately ($0.282$). We read this as the operational point of the
paper: a calibration number measured on in-distribution options says very little
about what the model will report when the option list is degenerate, and a
production caller thresholding on a returned probability is exposed to exactly
that case. The exposure is not hypothetical: confidence-based acceptance is the
mechanism of the cascade designs now proposed for this class
\citep{li2026jevjudge,ibrahim2026evaluating}, and a degenerate option list is a
routine consequence of an upstream bug, an empty retrieval or a mis-specified
schema rather than an adversarial construction.

A side-check comes with it: McNemar between the two open arms under \textsc{o4}
is not significant ($p = 0.76$), so the instrument's null behaves identically in
both.

Table~\ref{tab:order} and Figure~\ref{fig:order} give a clean dissociation: a
$7.8\times$ difference in answer stability and a $12\times$ difference in
probability stability, tracking the scoring architecture rather than model size,
backbone or capability. Lod-lille's design claim --- each option attends only to
the shared state, its question and itself from the same start position --- is
very nearly borne out. Laya-ml's option-marker scorer is not, confirming an
upstream issue report at scale.



The residual positional bias follows (Figure~\ref{fig:position}). With the gold
placed uniformly, Laya-ml selects slot B 37.2\% of the time against 14.6\% for
slot A, and its accuracy is $0.152$ when the gold sits in A against $0.403$ in B
--- a $2.6\times$ swing from position alone.

\paragraph{Methodological consequence.} Any evaluation of this class that fixes
option order is partly measuring position. All results here randomise order per
item, seeded by item id, and are averages over position rather than measurements
at one of them; we suggest this as a default for the class, not a robustness
check.

\subsection{Options are read as a bag of words}
\label{sec:bagofwords}

The \textsc{o}-series ablates the option text while holding the idiom fixed
(Figure~\ref{fig:options}). Two readings matter. First, truncating each option to
six words and \emph{scrambling the words inside it} cost almost nothing: Laya-ml
retains $0.967$ and $0.925$ of its signal, Lod-lille $0.909$ and $0.948$,
Jev-1.13 is flat to within noise. Whatever all three models are doing on the
option side is close to order-free lexical matching. Second, the drop to the grey
semantics-free bar is the only large one in the figure, which is what certifies
the instrument: the task really is measuring option semantics, it is just
measuring them as a bag.

This converges with \citet{sun2026typesafe}, who hold the option definitions
fixed and permute which name is bound to which, and find the decision follows
the name. We hold the names fixed and destroy the internal structure of the
text, and find the decision barely moves. The two manipulations are
complementary halves of the same claim: within an option, these models are
reading lexical identity rather than composed meaning, and the typed interface
reports a well-formed, high-confidence answer either way.


\subsection{Neither open model treats the idiom as a unit}
\label{sec:unit}

E3 perturbs the idiom while holding the option list fixed
(Figure~\ref{fig:unit}). This is the experiment that replaces token attribution:
since an idiom has no word-level meaning to attribute to, we instead ask whether
the model behaves as though it stores the expression as a unit. A model that did
would collapse when the unit is broken.

Neither does. Word-shuffling an idiom leaves Laya-ml's accuracy statistically
unchanged ($0.158 \rightarrow 0.168$) and reversal actually helps slightly
($0.180$); Lod-lille moves within noise on its 180 items. Answer agreement with
the intact form under shuffling is $0.742$ for Laya-ml and $0.917$ for Lod-lille
--- Lod-lille is more \emph{stable}, consistent with \S\ref{sec:order}, while
being no more \emph{compositional}.

The one manipulation that clearly helps both is embedding the idiom in its gold
carrier sentence: $+0.080$ and $+0.117$. Context, not the idiom string, carries
the usable signal --- which is itself the diagnosis. These models are not
retrieving a stored figurative entry; they are matching whatever lexical material
is available, and a carrier sentence simply supplies more of it.

That cuts both ways, and \citet{xu2026jevout} supply the other edge: short,
fluent, task-preserving context flips an initially correct decision 61.4\% of
the time. Our gold carrier sentences are the benign case of the same mechanism.
A model whose answer improves when helpful context is appended is, by the same
token, a model whose answer moves when context is appended at all --- and
nothing in the interface distinguishes the two.

\subsection{The failure localises to Bangla}
\label{sec:crosslingual}

Holding the model fixed and swapping only the task language
(Figure~\ref{fig:crosslingual}) moves every arm by a large margin: Laya-ml
$+0.246$, Lod-lille $+0.380$, Jev-1.13 $+0.233$ (all McNemar $p < 0.001$).
Lod-lille reaches $0.864$ in English against $0.484$ in Bangla, so the figurative
concepts are largely present --- it simply cannot reach them through Bangla. Its
documentation states that training evaluation was English-only, and this is what
that looks like from outside the model.

Laya-ml's control arm closes the argument: its English-only checkpoint run on
Bengali script sits at $0.283$, barely above chance, which reproduces a
documented failure mode and rules out the possibility that the Bangla result is
an artifact of the scorer rather than of the language.

\section{Discussion}

Three of our four claims are properties of the \emph{class}, established by
replication across architecturally unrelated models. Literal capture,
anti-diagnostic confidence and the no-signal control all reproduce across an
encoder scorer and a decoder scorer, and the commercial arm --- which escapes
the first two in English --- fails the third most severely. Option-order
dependence is the informative exception: it tracks the scoring architecture, and
the architecture claiming order-independence very nearly delivers it.

For practitioners: a returned probability should not be thresholded without
measuring what the model returns on a degenerate option list; a benchmark score
is not evidence against literal capture, since the two move in opposite
directions; and an evaluation that fixes option order is partly reporting
position.

Against the current literature this is a boundary condition, not a rebuttal: the
cost and latency advantages are established \citep{tang2026audit,rao2026rubric},
and what we add is that the property the cascade designs depend on degrades
first, and silently. Three concurrent findings point the same way --- the
decision follows an option's \emph{name} rather than its bound definition
\citep{sun2026typesafe}, benign context flips a majority of correct decisions
\citep{xu2026jevout}, and here confidence rises as accuracy falls. The typed
interface guarantees a well-formed answer and nothing about whether it was
arrived at for a reason, so each failure is invisible to a caller who only
checks that the output validates.

\section{Conclusion}

We audited three decision models on Bangla idioms using only interventions on
the option set, a method that requires no new annotation and that remains
available when a model emits no text and its weights are closed. Inserting an
idiom's own expert literal gloss drives all open arms far below chance, and the
model that is stronger on every other condition is the more captured one, so
literal capture is not something capability removes. Reported confidence rises
as accuracy falls under the same shift, and on a semantics-free option list the
best-calibrated arm reports $0.815$ confidence at chance accuracy --- which puts
a boundary on the confidence-gated cascade designs now being built on this model
class. Option-order sensitivity separates the two scoring architectures cleanly,
$0.837$ against $0.107$, and the failure throughout localises to Bangla rather
than to figurativeness. We release the instrument, the per-item outputs and the
analysis code so the same audit can be run on other models and other languages.

\section*{Limitations}

All three models are evaluated zero-shot; nothing here speaks to a fine-tuned
model of any of these families, and the open arms' documentation is explicit that
base checkpoints are weak on typed-decision benchmarks.

Laya-ml's run had its per-bucket temperatures unset, so its ECE figures are raw.
Lod-lille ships no temperature by design, so both open arms are effectively
untempered and comparable on that axis; Jev-1.13's internal calibration is
unknown to us.

Lod-lille ran a fixed 10\% subset (888 items), so its intervals are roughly
$\pm 3$ points and its stratified cells are thin. Appendix~\ref{app:subset}
bounds the subset bias at $0.016$ and shows it changes no sign or ordering. E3
on the Lod-lille arm is 180 items and several of its perturbation contrasts are
not individually significant. E7 covers 992 / 299 / 300 items rather than the
full sets.

The cross-model \LAR{} comparison in \S\ref{sec:capture} is confounded with
language, as Figure~\ref{fig:matrix} states explicitly; the two missing cells are
the experiment that would resolve it and we did not run them. The shared literal
pool is also not a neutral sample of the corpus.

Distractors are drawn from the same corpus, so difficulty is corpus-relative
rather than absolute. Jev-1.13 is a commercial endpoint and may change without
notice; our results are a snapshot, and we report the exact call count and cost
in Appendix~\ref{app:repro} so the snapshot is at least auditable.

Finally, no figure in this paper contains Bangla glyphs: the plotting toolchain
available to us could not shape Bengali script. All categories are named in Latin
script and qualitative Bangla examples are confined to the text.

\section*{Ethics Statement}

The corpus is a published, expert-annotated resource of idioms \citep{sakhawat2026bagdhara}; it contains no
personal data and no content about identifiable individuals. Our interventions
rewrite option lists and perturb idiom strings, and produce no new annotation and
no generated text.

The work audits two openly released models and one commercial API. We report
failure modes in all three, including the vendor's. We have taken care to
separate what is measured from what is inferred --- in particular,
Figure~\ref{fig:matrix} marks the cells we did not run rather than letting a
favourable contrast stand unqualified, and \S\ref{sec:capture} states the pool
selection effect against our own cleanest number. Commercial endpoints are
mutable; readers should treat the Jev-1.13 results as dated rather than as a
standing property of the product.

Bangla is under-served relative to its speaker population, and we think an audit
that locates a failure in the language rather than in the task is the more useful
kind to publish. We release the instrument, all per-item outputs and the analysis
code so the measurements can be repeated on other models and other languages.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}

The two open arms ran on a single T4 GPU, Laya-ml in \texttt{bf16} at 27.2\,ms
per call over 8{,}876 items and Lod-lille in \texttt{fp32} at 86.1\,ms per call
over a fixed 888-item subset. The commercial arm consumed 56{,}533 API calls at a
total cost of \$0.93. All intervals are 95\% percentile bootstrap over items with
2{,}000 resamples at a fixed seed; ECE uses 15 equal-width bins; paired tests are
McNemar's exact test. Per-item outputs, the analysis code, the figure sources and
the exact plotted values for every figure are released with the paper.
